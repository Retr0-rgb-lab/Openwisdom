## Keep four judgments separate

Never collapse:

| Judgment | Question |
| --- | --- |
| Claim truth | Does the proposition correspond to the facts? |
| Argument quality | Do the offered premises support the conclusion? |
| Rhetorical conduct | Does the presentation clarify, distract, pressure, or manipulate? |
| Bias hypothesis | What psychological process might have produced a systematic distortion? |

A true claim can be defended badly. A valid argument can be unsound. A speaker can be rude while correct. A polished argument can be false. A bias-consistent pattern does not establish a person's motive.

## Claim atomization

Split complex prose at conjunctions, quantifiers, causal verbs, comparisons, and normative terms. For each unit record:

- Exact wording.
- Clear proposition.
- Type: descriptive, definitional, causal, predictive, evaluative, prescriptive, legal, esthetic, psychological, or mixed.
- Scope: who, what, where, when, population, and comparison class.
- Quantifier: all, none, most, some, typical, often, can, must.
- Modality: actual, necessary, possible, probable, permitted, required.
- Epistemic strength: certain, likely, suspected, alleged.
- Role: premise, intermediate conclusion, final conclusion, concession, example, or rhetoric.
- Verification status and source.

Do not treat a question, command, exclamation, metaphor, or expression of preference as a factual argument unless a proposition is reasonably implied.

## Argument reconstruction

### Charitable reconstruction

1. Identify the main conclusion.
2. List explicit premises.
3. Add only the minimum plausible suppressed premises needed to make the reasoning coherent.
4. Preserve qualifications and context.
5. Distinguish explanation from argument: an explanation assumes the event and asks why; an argument tries to establish that it occurred or is true.
6. Identify linked, convergent, serial, or mutually supporting premises.
7. State alternative readings when the original is genuinely ambiguous.

Never improve an argument by secretly adding disputed facts. Label a charitable bridge as implicit and test whether the author would reasonably accept it.

### Argument type

- Deductive: Premises purport to guarantee the conclusion.
- Inductive generalization: Sample supports a population claim.
- Statistical syllogism: Population rate supports a case probability.
- Abductive: Evidence is claimed to be best explained by a hypothesis.
- Causal: Exposure or action is claimed to produce an outcome.
- Analogical: Similarities are claimed to support a further similarity.
- Testimonial: A source's report supports a claim.
- Practical: Facts, goals, constraints, and alternatives support an action.
- Normative: Facts plus a value standard support good, bad, should, or rights claims.

Evaluate an argument by the standard it actually claims. Do not fault a modest induction for failing to be deductively certain.

## Deductive reasoning

### Validity and soundness

- Valid: No case has all premises true and the conclusion false.
- Sound: Valid with true premises.

For a suspected formal defect:

1. Symbolize only enough structure to test the inference.
2. Preserve quantifier and modal scope.
3. Supply a countermodel if invalid.
4. Test whether a plausible missing premise repairs it.
5. Evaluate the repaired premise independently.

### Common valid forms

- Modus ponens: If P then Q; P; therefore Q.
- Modus tollens: If P then Q; not Q; therefore not P.
- Hypothetical syllogism: If P then Q; if Q then R; therefore if P then R.
- Disjunctive syllogism: P or Q; not P; therefore Q.
- Categorical syllogisms that satisfy distribution and quantity rules.

### Formal audit

Check:

- Affirming the consequent.
- Denying the antecedent.
- Invalid conversion.
- Inclusive versus exclusive disjunction.
- Undistributed middle.
- Illicit major or minor.
- Four terms or an equivocal middle.
- Existential import.
- Quantifier shift.
- Negation and contrary-versus-contradictory confusion.
- Modal scope.
- Inconsistent premises.

Invalidity establishes that the premises do not guarantee the conclusion, not that the conclusion is false.

## Induction, abduction, analogy, and testimony

### Inductive generalization

Ask:

- What is the target population?
- How was the sample selected?
- Is the sample large and varied enough for the claim?
- Are nonresponse, attrition, survivorship, or measurement errors material?
- Does the conclusion preserve the observed scope and uncertainty?

One case can establish existence or refute a universal claim. It rarely establishes prevalence or average effect.

### Abduction

Compare hypotheses by:

- Fit to all material evidence, not a selected subset.
- Explanatory scope and specificity.
- Compatibility with established knowledge.
- Independent testability.
- Need for ad hoc auxiliary assumptions.
- Predictive novelty.
- Plausible mechanism.
- Performance against serious alternatives.

Do not automatically choose the most vivid, simple, intentional, or morally satisfying story.

### Analogy

List:

- Source and target.
- Shared properties.
- Property projected.
- Causal relevance of similarities.
- Relevant differences.
- Number and diversity of source cases.

An analogy illustrates unless the shared structure makes the projected property more likely.

### Testimony and expert judgment

Rational division of cognitive labor is necessary. Expert reliance is not automatically an appeal to authority. Evaluate:

- Relevant expertise and scope.
- Direct access to the evidence.
- Method and transparency.
- Track record and calibration.
- Independence and conflicts.
- Agreement or disagreement among qualified specialists.
- Whether the field has reliable methods for this question.
- Whether the testimony is being used as defeasible evidence or infallible decree.

For factual claims, authority affects evidential weight but does not make reality conform.

## Evidence and source quality

### Evidence ladder

The appropriate ordering depends on the field, but generally prefer:

1. Direct observation, original records, raw data, artifacts, and primary documents.
2. Well-designed studies, official analyses, or technical reports with inspectable methods.
3. Systematic syntheses of multiple independent sources.
4. Qualified expert interpretation.
5. Reputable reporting linked to its basis.
6. Advocacy, editorial, or secondary summaries.
7. Anonymous assertion, viral repetition, or unsourced AI output.

This is not mechanical. A forged primary document is worse than a careful secondary analysis; official status does not eliminate incentives or error.

### Source audit

- Provenance: Who produced it and how close are they to the evidence?
- Competence: Are they qualified for this specific claim?
- Independence: Are apparently multiple sources repeating one origin?
- Method: How were observations selected, measured, and analyzed?
- Incentives: What financial, political, legal, reputational, or ideological pressures exist?
- Date: Is the claim time-sensitive or superseded?
- Context: Is text quoted accurately and fully?
- Corroboration: Do independent methods converge?
- Correctability: Does the source publish data, uncertainty, and corrections?

Do not commit the genetic fallacy: source defects change testimonial weight, not the independently testable truth value by themselves.

### Current events and live facts

When tools permit:

1. Search the exact claim and key terms.
2. Open the primary source rather than relying on snippets.
3. Compare event date with publication date.
4. Corroborate decisive disputed facts independently.
5. Separate reporting from commentary.
6. State what remains unverified.

An Objectivist article can supply a valuable interpretation while its factual premises still require independent verification.

## Causal reasoning

### Causal specification

Define:

- Cause, exposure, intervention, or policy.
- Outcome.
- Population and setting.
- Time window and lag.
- Counterfactual: What would have happened to comparable units without the cause?

### Causal checklist

1. Did the proposed cause precede the outcome?
2. Do cause and outcome covary under relevant conditions?
3. Could the direction be reversed or reciprocal?
4. What common causes affect both?
5. How were units selected into exposure and observation?
6. Was a collider, mediator, or post-treatment variable conditioned on?
7. Is there a plausible mechanism consistent with entity identities?
8. Do intervention, natural experiment, dose-response, discontinuity, or repeated evidence support the claim?
9. Are alternative causal structures tested?
10. Does the evidence transport to this population, time, dose, and implementation?

### Causal evidence

No single method is universally required. Randomization can control confounding but may have compliance, measurement, or external-validity limits. Observational designs can be strong with natural experiments, longitudinal structure, negative controls, sensitivity analysis, or convergent mechanisms.

Do not:

- Infer causation from sequence or correlation alone.
- Demand a randomized trial when impossible or unethical and other evidence is strong.
- Treat mechanism as proof of effect size.
- Treat prediction as causal identification.
- Ignore feedback, adaptation, incentives, or multiple causes.
- Confuse necessary, sufficient, enabling, and contributing conditions.

### Regression and selection

Extreme observations often move closer to the average on remeasurement even without intervention. Survivorship, attrition, collider conditioning, and treatment self-selection can create or reverse associations.

For consequential causal work, consult domain methods rather than relying solely on fallacy names.

## Statistical and probabilistic reasoning

### Extract the quantities

For each numeric claim identify:

| Field | Value or question |
| --- | --- |
| Population | Who or what is the claim about? |
| Sample | Who or what was measured? |
| Sampling process | Random, complete, convenience, self-selected, matched? |
| Numerator | Events or successes counted? |
| Denominator | Eligible units or exposure? |
| Time window | When and for how long? |
| Comparison | Baseline or control? |
| Absolute effect | Difference in actual rates or amounts? |
| Relative effect | Ratio or percent change? |
| Uncertainty | Interval, error, model, or range? |
| Base rate | Prior or background frequency? |
| Missingness | Who or what is absent and why? |
| Multiplicity | How many outcomes, groups, or models were tried? |
| Claim type | Descriptive, predictive, or causal? |

### Probability

Check:

- P(data given hypothesis) versus P(hypothesis given data).
- Prior/base rate and likelihood.
- Conditional direction.
- Independence.
- Conjunction and disjunction.
- Numerator and denominator.
- Expected value, variance, downside, and tail risk.
- Calibration across repeated predictions.

The gambler's fallacy applies only to independent, stable trials. Card removal, equipment changes, learning, fatigue, strategy, contagion, or other dependencies can make history relevant.

### Statistical interpretation

Do not equate:

- Statistical significance with importance or a large effect.
- Nonsignificance with no effect.
- One significant result and one nonsignificant result with a significant difference between them.
- A p-value with the probability the hypothesis is true.
- A confidence interval with a posterior probability without the relevant model.
- Correlation or model coefficient with causal effect.
- Odds ratio with risk ratio when outcomes are common.
- Mean with a complete distribution.
- Relative risk with absolute practical impact.

Check multiple testing, optional stopping, HARKing, p-hacking, publication bias, selective endpoints, overfitting, data leakage, extrapolation, and false precision.

### Presentation

Inspect axes, truncation, bins, baselines, cumulative versus per-period values, log scales, inflation adjustment, population adjustment, time-window selection, and omitted uncertainty.

## Normative and practical reasoning

### Normative bridge

An is-to-ought analysis must state:

1. Facts about the kind of entity and the alternative it faces.
2. The chosen or objectively grounded standard of value.
3. The causal relationship between the proposed action and that standard.
4. Relevant alternatives, context, and time horizon.
5. The action principle or evaluation that follows.

Objectivism grounds values in the requirements of living organisms and human life; it does not infer goodness from mere naturalness. "Natural" can identify causal facts but is not itself the value standard.

### Practical argument

Map:

- Goal and why it is a value.
- Feasible options, including delay or no action.
- Constraints and rights.
- Direct, indirect, and opportunity costs.
- Uncertainty and reversibility.
- Incentives and second-order effects.
- Failure conditions.

Do not optimize a metric that is only a proxy for the actual value. Do not count sunk costs as future benefits. Do not treat one best general principle as selecting a unique personal option when several rational alternatives exist.

### Ethics and politics bridge

Before recommending law, ask:

- Is the conduct merely false, foolish, offensive, or immoral?
- Does it initiate force, fraud, breach, or a rights violation?
- Is government action retaliatory and governed by objective law?
- What enforcement force does the policy itself require?

## Rhetoric and dialogue

Classify as rhetorical or dialectical rather than logical when appropriate:

- Loaded language.
- Ridicule or intimidation.
- Gish gallop.
- Repetition and slogans.
- Question evasion.
- Motte-and-bailey shifts.
- Whataboutism.
- Poisoning the well.
- Quote mining.
- Selective framing.
- Performative contradiction.

Rhetoric can accompany a sound argument. Identify whether it replaces, obscures, or merely emphasizes the reasoning.

## Steelman, repair, and conclusion

### Steelman

- Preserve the author's actual thesis, not a different better thesis.
- Add only plausible shared premises and label them.
- Narrow quantifiers to the evidence.
- Clarify definitions.
- Separate empirical and moral components.
- State the strongest serious objection.

### Repair ladder

1. Fix wording or ambiguity.
2. Restore omitted context or qualification.
3. Replace a weak premise with available evidence.
4. Narrow the conclusion.
5. Add a warranted bridge premise.
6. Change the inferential type or confidence.
7. Suspend or abandon the conclusion when no honest repair exists.

### Integrated conclusion

Report separately:

- Truth status of the main claim.
- Quality of the offered argument.
- Quality and completeness of evidence.
- Material fallacies or rhetoric.
- Objectivist consistency and derivation.
- Missing evidence and what would change the result.

Explain the defect first and name it second.
