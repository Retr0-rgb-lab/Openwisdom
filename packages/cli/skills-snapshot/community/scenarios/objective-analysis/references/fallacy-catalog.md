## Use rules

A fallacy charge has its own burden of proof. Before naming one:

1. Quote or identify the exact passage.
2. Reconstruct the inference charitably.
3. State the norm the inference violates.
4. Check the legitimate near-neighbor.
5. Explain the material effect.
6. Offer a repair.

Not every false statement is a fallacy. Not every rhetorical tactic is an inference. Invalid reasoning does not make its conclusion false. A named pattern can be reasonable when its critical conditions are satisfied.

Tags:

- YLFI: included in the Your Logical Fallacy Is set.
- Formal: invalid by logical form.
- Evidential: evidence is insufficient, selected, or misused.
- Rhetorical: a discourse tactic; classify as a fallacy only if it performs inferential work.
- Objectivist: an epistemic error emphasized or originated in Objectivist literature.

## The 24 Your Logical Fallacy Is categories

These independently written tests cover all 24 categories on [Your Logical Fallacy Is](https://yourlogicalfallacyis.com/). The site is an accessible starting point, not an exhaustive or infallible taxonomy.

| Fallacy | Confirm the defect | False-positive guard | Repair |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Straw man [YLFI] | A target position T is materially altered in scope, strength, qualification, or content into R, and refuting R is offered as refuting T. | Require an identifiable target and the strongest reasonable reading. Compression or good-faith misunderstanding alone is not enough. | Quote T, state the disputed transformation, and answer T. |
| False cause [YLFI] | A causal conclusion rests mainly on sequence, correlation, or co-occurrence without adequate control of alternatives. | Correlation can be causal evidence. Weigh design, timing, mechanism, background knowledge, and alternatives. | State the causal model and seek counterfactual or convergent evidence. |
| Appeal to emotion [YLFI] | A desired emotional response substitutes for evidence relevant to the conclusion. | Emotion, suffering, preferences, and risk can be relevant facts; emotional wording can accompany a sound argument. | State the factual and value premises separately. |
| Fallacy fallacy [YLFI] | "The offered argument for C is defective; therefore C is false." | The justified conclusion is often only that this argument does not establish C. | Reassess C using independent evidence. |
| Slippery slope [YLFI] | A is rejected because it allegedly leads to Z through one or more unsupported transitions. | Slopes can be sound when precedent, incentives, feedback, or mechanisms support each link. | Estimate and evidence each transition and cumulative risk. |
| Ad hominem [YLFI] | A trait of the speaker is treated as making the proposition false when the trait is irrelevant to its truth. | Expertise, honesty, access, conflict, and reliability can affect testimonial weight. | Evaluate the claim directly and source reliability separately. |
| Tu quoque [YLFI] | A criticism is dismissed because the critic is hypocritical or inconsistent. | Hypocrisy can matter to credibility, enforcement, or sincerity, but not the rule's truth. | Answer the criticism; evaluate hypocrisy as a separate claim. |
| Personal incredulity [YLFI] | "I cannot understand or imagine P; therefore P is false or unlikely." | A real contradiction or failure to make a claim intelligible can justify suspension. | Identify the missing knowledge or show the contradiction. |
| Special pleading [YLFI] | A rule is applied asymmetrically, often after counterevidence, without an independently relevant difference. | Legitimate exceptions rest on real distinctions and apply consistently. | State the rule and justify every exception before applying it. |
| Loaded question [YLFI] | A question embeds a disputed premise that a direct answer seems to concede. | The presupposition may already be established. | Split and test the presupposition before answering the remaining question. |
| Burden of proof [YLFI] | A claim is treated as established because opponents have not disproved it, or the claimant refuses the appropriate burden. | Negative claims can bear burdens; absence is evidence when expected evidence should be found. | Identify claimant, predicted evidence, access, stakes, and required support. |
| Ambiguity [YLFI] | The inference depends on a shift in word meaning, scope, referent, or syntax. | Ambiguity without inferential use is merely unclear. | Fix the meaning at each occurrence and retest. |
| Gambler's fallacy [YLFI] | Past outcomes in an independent stationary process are claimed to make a contrary outcome due. | First establish independence; cards, machines, performers, and opponents may create dependence. | Calculate the conditional probability under the actual process. |
| Bandwagon [YLFI] | Popularity or common practice is treated as sufficient evidence of truth or moral correctness. | Popularity matters to conventions, coordination, demand, and can reflect informed consensus. | Identify whether agreement is independent evidence or merely a head count. |
| Appeal to authority [YLFI] | An authority's assertion replaces evidence, especially outside scope or against stronger field evidence. | Qualified expert testimony and consensus are rational evidence under division of cognitive labor. | Check expertise, relevance, method, agreement, independence, and conflicts. |
| Composition or division [YLFI] | A property is transferred part-to-whole or whole-to-part without a valid aggregation or distribution rule. | Some properties are additive or distributive. | Identify whether the property is individual, collective, relational, or emergent. |
| No true Scotsman [YLFI] | A counterexample is excluded by an ad hoc purity definition whose function is to protect the generalization. | Definitions can be refined after discovering a real distinction. | Justify the distinction independently and apply it consistently. |
| Genetic fallacy [YLFI] | Origin or source is treated as determining a claim's truth or value. | Provenance bears on authenticity, contamination, and testimonial weight. | Separate source reliability from independently testable content. |
| Black-or-white [YLFI] | Non-exhaustive alternatives are presented as exhaustive. | P versus not-P is exhaustive; some decisions are genuinely binary. | Identify at least one live omitted alternative or dimension. |
| Begging the question [YLFI] | A premise's warrant depends on accepting the conclusion, or the disputed core is restated. | Validity is not circularity; shared background premises may be legitimate. | Trace support until it reaches independent evidence. |
| Appeal to nature [YLFI] | Natural or artificial status alone is used to infer good, bad, safe, just, or inevitable. | Natural facts can matter within an explicit life-based value or causal framework. | State the value standard and causal relationship. |
| Anecdotal [YLFI] | One or a few salient cases estimate a population rate or general causal effect. | Anecdotes can establish existence, refute a universal, and generate hypotheses. | Match the evidence to the claim and use representative data for prevalence. |
| Texas sharpshooter [YLFI] | Outcomes, groups, or windows are chosen after seeing data and presented as preplanned confirmation. | Exploratory patterns can become evidence through preregistration or independent replication. | Label exploration, correct for search, and replicate. |
| Middle ground [YLFI] | A midpoint is claimed true merely because it lies between opposing positions. | Compromise can be practical under uncertainty without being epistemic truth. | Evaluate each position against evidence and the decision goal. |

## Formal deductive defects

Apply only when the argument purports to guarantee its conclusion. Supply a countermodel where useful.

| Defect | Invalid form or test | Guard or repair |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Affirming the consequent | If P then Q; Q; therefore P. | Add evidence excluding other causes of Q or use a probabilistic conclusion. |
| Denying the antecedent | If P then Q; not P; therefore not Q. | Q may have other sufficient conditions. |
| Affirming a disjunct | P or Q; P; therefore not Q, when "or" is inclusive. | Valid only if exclusivity is established. |
| Invalid conversion | All A are B; therefore all B are A. | Convert only where class identity or a valid conversion rule applies. |
| Undistributed middle | A and C are each related to B without establishing their relation to each other. | Check categorical term distribution. |
| Illicit major | The conclusion distributes the major term when its premise did not. | Repair the premise or narrow the conclusion. |
| Illicit minor | The conclusion distributes the minor term when its premise did not. | Repair the premise or narrow the conclusion. |
| Four-term syllogism | The argument has four terms, often through equivocation. | Stabilize the middle term and reconstruct. |
| Existential fallacy | Existence is inferred from universal premises that do not assert any members. | Add an existential premise when warranted. |
| Quantifier shift | Every x has some y becomes one y for every x, or the reverse. | Preserve the order and scope of quantifiers. |
| Scope fallacy | Negation, modality, or a qualifier changes scope across the inference. | Parenthesize the logical structure. |
| Modal fallacy | Possible becomes actual, actual becomes necessary, or necessary consequent becomes necessary antecedent. | State modality for each proposition and world/context. |
| Masked-man or intensional substitution | Co-referential terms are substituted inside belief, necessity, or knowledge contexts where substitution is not truth preserving. | Separate object identity from the subject's knowledge. |
| Contrary/contradictory confusion | Not all A are B is treated as no A are B, or similar. | Use explicit quantifiers and negation. |
| Inconsistent premises | Premises jointly cannot be true, making any conclusion formally derivable. | Locate and resolve the contradiction rather than celebrating validity. |
| Non sequitur | The conclusion does not follow and no narrower named form captures the failure. | State the missing bridge instead of using the label alone. |

## Relevance and diversion

| Defect | Detection test | Legitimate near-neighbor |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Irrelevant conclusion / ignoratio elenchi | Reasons support a different conclusion from the one at issue. | A subsidiary conclusion can matter if its connection is stated. |
| Red herring | Discussion is diverted to a topic that does not change the disputed claim. | Context can reveal a premise that genuinely reframes the issue. |
| Weak man | A real but unusually weak version of an opposing view is treated as representative. | Critiquing a weak published argument is fair if not generalized. |
| Hollow man | A dubious position is invented and attacked without an identifiable proponent or evidence of prevalence. | A hypothetical position can test a principle if labeled hypothetical. |
| Poisoning the well | Negative source information preemptively discredits any argument regardless of content. | Advance disclosure of a material conflict can be rational. |
| Circumstantial ad hominem | Circumstances or incentives are offered as refutation. | Incentives can affect source reliability and evidence selection. |
| Guilt by association | A view is rejected because a disliked person or group shares it. | Association can be evidence of coordination or provenance, not truth. |
| Motive fallacy | A possible motive is treated as disproving the claim. | Motive can matter to credibility, intent, or legal elements. |
| Appeal to consequences | A proposition is called true or false because believing it has desirable or undesirable effects. | Consequences are central to action recommendations, not factual truth alone. |
| Appeal to fear | Fear is induced in place of establishing probability or harm. | Serious evidenced danger should produce concern. |
| Appeal to pity | Sympathy replaces the relevant standard or evidence. | Suffering can be a material value or remedy consideration. |
| Appeal to ridicule | Mockery substitutes for a demonstration of absurdity or contradiction. | Humor can expose a demonstrated implication. |
| Appeal to flattery | Praise or identity affirmation substitutes for reasons. | Respectful framing can facilitate communication. |
| Appeal to spite or envy | Hostility toward a person or group is used as a reason for a conclusion. | Evidence of wrongdoing remains relevant regardless of emotion. |
| Appeal to force / ad baculum | Threat is used to induce assent rather than action compliance. | A warning about an actual consequence is not a proof but may guide action. |
| Appeal to tradition | Longevity is treated as proof of truth or goodness. | Survival can provide defeasible evidence of function or coordination. |
| Appeal to novelty | Newness is treated as proof of superiority or truth. | New evidence or technology can create actual advantages. |
| Appeal to wealth | Wealth or market success is treated as proof of truth or virtue. | Success can be evidence of some competence under specified conditions. |
| Appeal to poverty | Lack of wealth is treated as proof of purity, truth, or victimhood. | Material deprivation can be relevant to opportunity and harm. |
| Relative privation | A problem is dismissed because a worse problem exists. | Priority decisions legitimately compare scale and urgency. |
| Two wrongs make a right | Another wrong is offered as moral authorization for this wrong. | Proportionate retaliation is a distinct principle requiring prior aggression. |
| Whataboutism | A criticism is evaded by shifting to another party's misconduct. | Comparative consistency can be examined after answering the original claim. |
| Argument by slogan | A compressed phrase substitutes for premises and definitions. | A slogan can summarize an argument already supplied. |
| Proof by assertion or repetition | Restatement is treated as increased evidence. | Independent repeated observations can add evidence; repeated claims do not. |
| Tone fallacy | Tone is treated as settling the content. | Tone can affect clarity, intent, threat, credibility, and practical persuasion. |

## Illegitimate presumption

| Defect | Detection test | Guard or repair |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Circular support | A support chain returns to the original claim without independent evidence. | Mutual coherence is useful only when at least part is independently anchored. |
| Complex question | Multiple questions or premises are bundled so one answer appears to settle all. | Separate components and answer each. |
| Suppressed evidence | Known or readily relevant contrary evidence is omitted from an apparently comprehensive case. | Every summary omits detail; show materiality and expected inclusion. |
| Moving the goalposts | Success criteria change after the original standard is met. | A new test is legitimate if new evidence reveals the first was insufficient; admit the update. |
| Ad hoc rescue | An exception is invented solely to protect a claim and has no independent support. | Auxiliary hypotheses are legitimate when testable and independently motivated. |
| Immunizing strategy | A claim is insulated from every possible counterexample. | Some claims have indirect tests; state what evidence could count. |
| Argument from ignorance | P is true because not-P is unproved, or vice versa. | Absence matters when the hypothesis predicts detectable evidence. |
| Accident | A general rule is applied despite an exceptional condition material to its purpose. | State the rule's contextual conditions. |
| Converse accident | An exceptional case is generalized into the ordinary rule. | Use representative cases. |
| False equivalence | Cases with superficial similarity are treated as equal despite material differences. | Identify the governing dimension and compare on it. |
| Continuum fallacy | Because no sharp boundary exists, meaningful distinctions or endpoints are denied. | Thresholds can be objective and contextual even on a continuum. |
| Perfect-solution / nirvana fallacy | A real option is rejected because it is imperfect, often against an imaginary ideal. | Compare feasible alternatives, including status quo and costs. |
| Proving too much | A premise would establish implausibly broad conclusions the proponent rejects. | Narrow or reject the premise. |
| Inconsistent standards | Like cases receive different evidential or moral standards without relevant difference. | State the difference or use one standard. |
| False premise | The inference rests on a factually false premise. | This is a defect but not always a named fallacy; verify before labeling. |
| Unsupported hidden premise | A necessary bridge is unspoken and lacks support. | Add and assess the premise explicitly. |
| Appeal to possibility | Mere conceivable compatibility is treated as evidence that a scenario is live or likely. | Require positive grounding and causal compatibility. |
| Argument from silence | Failure to mention something is treated as evidence it did not exist. | Silence can matter when the source was expected and able to report it. |
| Self-sealing explanation | Every outcome, including failure, is reinterpreted as confirmation. | Specify discriminating predictions. |
| Kafka trap | Denial of an accusation is treated as proof of the accusation. | Seek independent evidence; denial has many explanations. |

## Ambiguity and conceptual defects

| Defect | Detection test | Repair |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Equivocation | A key word changes meaning between premises and conclusion. | Substitute explicit definitions at each use. |
| Amphiboly | Syntax supports different readings and the inference trades on them. | Rewrite the sentence with explicit grammar. |
| Accent | Emphasis or selective quotation changes a statement's meaning. | Restore full emphasis and context. |
| Contextomy / quote mining | A quotation is detached from text that materially qualifies or reverses it. | Read and cite sufficient surrounding material. |
| Referent shift | A pronoun, group, or label changes the entity it denotes. | Name the entity each time. |
| Use-mention confusion | A word or representation is confused with its referent. | Distinguish the term, concept, and object. |
| Etymological fallacy | Historical word origin is treated as fixing current meaning or reality. | Determine actual usage and referents. |
| Persuasive definition | A definition embeds praise, blame, or the disputed conclusion. | Define by class and essential distinction. |
| Reification / hypostatization | An abstraction is treated as a concrete acting entity. | Reduce to entities, actions, and relationships. |
| Category mistake | A property or operation is assigned to the wrong logical or ontological kind. | Identify the proper category and bearer. |
| Motte-and-bailey | A strong controversial claim is advanced, then replaced by a weak defensible one under challenge, and later restored. | Freeze each proposition and evaluate separately. |
| Vagueness exploited as precision | A vague predicate is used as if it supplied a sharp measurement or boundary. | Operationalize or acknowledge the range. |
| Loaded language | Evaluative wording pre-judges a factual classification. | Replace with neutral description, then justify evaluation. |
| Euphemism or dysphemism | Word choice hides or exaggerates material properties. | State the concrete action and consequence. |
| Weasel wording | Unnamed sources or elastic qualifiers create an appearance of evidence without accountability. | Name source, quantity, scope, and confidence. |
| Definist fallacy | A stipulative definition is used to settle a substantive dispute about the world. | Separate label choice from factual classification. |
| Package deal [Objectivist] | Different phenomena are bundled and attributes transferred. | Split by essential differences. |
| Frozen abstraction [Objectivist] | One narrow unit replaces the full concept. | Restore the open-ended range. |
| Anti-concept [Objectivist] | An invalid term erases a needed valid concept. | Recover referents and distinctions. |

## Weak induction and evidence

| Defect | Detection test | Guard or repair |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hasty generalization | Sample is too small or narrow for the population conclusion. | Match scope and uncertainty to sample. |
| Unrepresentative sample | Selection differs materially from target population. | Use probability sampling, weighting, or qualify transport. |
| Convenience or volunteer bias | Availability or self-selection changes relevant traits. | Compare participants with nonparticipants. |
| Survivorship bias | Only units that passed a selection or survival process are observed. | Reconstruct the full starting cohort. |
| Nonresponse or attrition bias | Missing units differ in outcome-related ways. | Analyze missingness and sensitivity. |
| Cherry-picking | Supporting evidence is selected while comparable contrary evidence is ignored. | Define inclusion criteria before results. |
| Suppressed counterexample | A material known exception is omitted from a universal or strong claim. | Revise quantifier or principle. |
| Weak analogy | Similarities do not bear causally on the projected property or differences dominate. | State the relevant structure and disanalogies. |
| Misleading vividness | A memorable case receives more weight than representative evidence. | Use rates and full distributions. |
| Single-study dependence | A conclusion outruns one study's design, population, or uncertainty. | Seek replication and synthesis. |
| Single-source dependence | Several claims trace to one uncorroborated origin. | Check source independence. |
| Source laundering | Repetition through secondary outlets creates false appearance of corroboration. | Trace each claim to origin. |
| Hypothesis-confirmation confusion | Exploratory pattern is presented as a confirmed prior hypothesis. | Separate discovery and validation data. |
| Overgeneralization | A result is exported beyond time, population, dose, setting, or construct. | State transport assumptions. |
| Ecological inference | Group-level relationship is assigned to individuals. | Obtain individual-level data or model cross-level relation. |
| Atomistic inference | Individual-level relationship is assigned to groups. | Analyze aggregation and composition. |
| Absence-of-evidence misuse | No evidence is treated as no effect without considering search power. | Ask what evidence should be visible if true. |
| Appeal to unrepresentative experience | Personal professional experience is treated as exhaustive. | Use it as informed testimony with explicit selection limits. |
| Observer expectancy | Measurements or interpretations are influenced by expectations. | Blind, standardize, or independently code. |
| Measurement substitution | An easy proxy silently replaces the target construct. | Validate the proxy and state its limits. |

## Causal errors

| Defect | Detection test | Repair |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Post hoc | Earlier event is called the cause mainly because it preceded the outcome. | Compare counterfactual and background trend. |
| Cum hoc | Co-occurrence is treated as causation. | Test direction, confounding, and mechanism. |
| Reverse causation | Effect plausibly produces the alleged cause. | Use temporal and intervention evidence. |
| Confounding | A common cause produces both exposure and outcome. | Design or adjust using a defensible causal model. |
| Collider bias | Conditioning on a common effect creates a spurious association. | Do not condition on colliders; diagram selection. |
| Post-treatment bias | A mediator or treatment consequence is controlled as if preexisting. | Define the estimand and temporal causal graph. |
| Selection bias | Inclusion depends on exposure, outcome, or their causes. | Model or redesign selection. |
| Monocausal oversimplification | A multifactorial outcome is attributed to one cause without bounding its contribution. | Distinguish contributing, necessary, sufficient, and enabling causes. |
| Necessary/sufficient confusion | A necessary condition is treated as sufficient, or vice versa. | State the causal role explicitly. |
| Regression neglect | Movement from an extreme toward average is credited to intervention. | Use control/comparison and repeated baselines. |
| Feedback neglect | Reciprocal causation is represented as one-way. | Model dynamic loops and timing. |
| Common-trend fallacy | Two trending series correlate because both move with time. | Detrend and test plausible causal variation. |
| Mechanism-only inference | A plausible mechanism is treated as proof of actual effect or size. | Measure outcomes. |
| Effect-only inference | An association is declared causal without a coherent intervention or mechanism. | Specify causal model and identification. |
| Proxy-as-cause | A marker or prediction feature is treated as an intervention target. | Ask what changing the proxy would do. |
| Counterfactual failure | Outcome after action is compared with nothing or the wrong alternative. | Define a credible no-action or alternative-action baseline. |
| Transportability failure | Causal result is exported to a different population or implementation without support. | Analyze effect modifiers and context. |
| Slippery causal chain | A multistep sequence is asserted without evidence for links. | Evidence and multiply conditional risks. |
| Intervention mismatch | Evidence for one dose, form, or implementation is used for another. | Match the actual intervention. |
| Predictive-causal confusion | A variable predicts outcome but changing it need not change outcome. | Separate prediction from intervention. |

## Statistical and probabilistic errors

| Defect | Detection test | Repair |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Base-rate neglect | Case evidence is evaluated without the background frequency. | Apply the relevant prior/reference class. |
| Inverse probability | P(data given H) is confused with P(H given data). | Use Bayes' rule with alternatives and priors. |
| Prosecutor's fallacy | A rare match under innocence is treated as a tiny probability of innocence. | Compare likelihoods and base rates. |
| Defense-attorney's fallacy | A small expected number of random matches is used to dismiss strong case-specific evidence. | Evaluate total evidence and likelihood ratio. |
| Conjunction fallacy | P(A and B) is judged greater than P(A). | Enforce the conjunction rule. |
| Denominator neglect | Raw counts or vivid numerators replace rates. | State numerator and denominator. |
| Ratio bias | Larger raw numerator feels more likely despite a lower proportion. | Compare normalized probabilities. |
| Sample-size neglect | Small and large samples are treated as equally stable. | Model sampling variability. |
| Law of small numbers | Tiny samples are expected to mirror population proportions. | Widen uncertainty and collect more data. |
| Clustering illusion | Random clusters are treated as meaningful without a specified test. | Compare with expected cluster distribution. |
| Hot-hand error | Streaks are dismissed or asserted without testing dependence and selection. | Model the actual sequence and selection rule. |
| Inverse gambler's fallacy | A rare observation is used to infer many prior trials without considering alternatives. | Use explicit priors and observation selection. |
| Regression to mean | Extreme follow-up change is misread as effect. | Use control and repeated measurement. |
| Multiple comparisons | One positive among many tests is treated at face value. | Correct or independently validate. |
| Look-elsewhere effect | Search over locations, groups, models, or windows is omitted. | Report search space and adjusted evidence. |
| P-hacking | Analysis choices are optimized for a desired threshold. | Preregister, disclose alternatives, replicate. |
| Optional stopping | Data collection ends when a favorable result appears without adjustment. | Use sequential methods or fixed rules. |
| HARKing | A post hoc hypothesis is presented as prior. | Label exploratory and validate anew. |
| Publication bias | Available literature overrepresents positive or novel results. | Seek registries, null results, and bias analysis. |
| Significance fallacy | Statistical significance is equated with truth, size, or importance. | Report effect, uncertainty, design, and practical meaning. |
| Nonsignificance-as-no-effect | Low power or uncertainty is treated as proof of zero. | Report interval and equivalence bounds. |
| Difference-in-significance | One subgroup is significant and another is not, so groups are said to differ. | Test the interaction directly. |
| Confidence-interval misreading | A frequentist interval is read as a posterior probability for this parameter. | State the procedure's coverage meaning or use an explicit Bayesian model. |
| Relative-risk inflation | A large percent change hides a tiny absolute change. | Report baseline and absolute effect. |
| Odds-risk confusion | Odds ratio is read as risk ratio. | Convert when assumptions permit. |
| Mean substitution | Average hides variance, skew, tails, or subgroup effects. | Show distribution and relevant quantiles. |
| False precision | More digits imply more certainty than data or model supports. | Round to justified precision and report uncertainty. |
| Misleading graph | Axes, bins, baselines, or scales distort magnitude or trend. | Replot with justified choices and full range/context. |
| Cherry-picked window | Start/end dates are chosen for a desired trend. | Show longer series and predeclared window. |
| Extrapolation | Model is projected beyond observed support. | Limit range or justify mechanism. |
| Overfitting | Model learns noise and performs poorly out of sample. | Use held-out validation and regularization. |
| Data leakage | Training or prediction uses information unavailable at decision time. | Enforce temporal and unit separation. |
| Pseudoreplication | Correlated observations are treated as independent units. | Model clustering and true experimental unit. |
| Missing-data neglect | Analysis assumes missingness is harmless without support. | Diagnose mechanism and sensitivity. |
| Measurement error neglect | Misclassification or noisy measures bias result. | Validate instruments and model error. |
| Selective endpoints | Only favorable outcomes or specifications are reported. | Require protocol and full outcome set. |
| Calibration-discrimination confusion | Ranking ability is treated as accurate probability estimation, or vice versa. | Evaluate both separately. |
| Expected-value tunnel | Mean payoff ignores variance, ruin, irreversibility, or tail risk. | Include distribution, constraints, and utility of outcomes. |

## Rhetorical and dialogue tactics

These may be objectionable without being fallacies. Identify their effect on inquiry.

| Tactic | Pattern | Response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Gish gallop | Many claims are delivered faster than they can be checked. | Select decisive claims, demand sources, and track burden. |
| Sealioning | Repetitive demands for proof continue after reasonable answers, often feigning neutrality. | State answered questions and a stopping rule; do not infer intent casually. |
| Question evasion | Response addresses a nearby issue instead of the question. | Restate the question and note the non-answer. |
| Motte-and-bailey | Strong and weak versions alternate opportunistically. | Label and freeze both propositions. |
| Whataboutism | Another wrong displaces the original issue. | Answer original first, then compare consistently. |
| Argument from intimidation [Objectivist] | Status or moral pressure substitutes for reasons. | Remove pressure and request evidence. |
| Proof by verbosity | Length and complexity create an appearance of rigor without a valid chain. | Extract premises and conclusion. |
| Deepity | An ambiguous sentence has one true trivial reading and one profound unsupported reading. | Separate readings. |
| Persuasive framing | Presentation changes response while leaving substance constant. | Rewrite in extensionally equivalent neutral frames. |
| Strategic ambiguity | Speaker benefits from multiple meanings and shifts under challenge. | Require explicit commitments. |
| Dog whistle allegation | Hidden meaning is asserted from a phrase without contextual evidence. | Seek audience, pattern, provenance, and behavior evidence. |
| Bulverism | A claim is assumed false and explained psychologically instead of refuted. | Establish truth status before causal psychology. |

## Severity and reporting

Use:

- Fatal: The offered inference cannot establish the conclusion and no plausible local repair remains.
- Material: The conclusion may survive, but a major premise, scope, or confidence requires repair.
- Limited: A secondary point, wording, or rhetorical move is defective without changing the core.
- Rhetorical only: Presentation impairs dialogue but does not itself defeat the inference.

Report:

- Name and class.
- Exact passage.
- Reconstructed inference.
- Violated standard.
- False-positive guard checked.
- Effect on the conclusion.
- Repair.
- Confidence.

Explain the defect first; name it second.
