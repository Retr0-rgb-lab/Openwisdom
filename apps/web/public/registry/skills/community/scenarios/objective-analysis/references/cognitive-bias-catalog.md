## Use rules

A cognitive bias is a systematic tendency to depart from a stated normative benchmark, such as logical validity, probabilistic coherence, calibration, evidential symmetry, expected value, truth-conducive inquiry, or successful goal pursuit.

Before naming a bias:

1. State the benchmark.
2. Identify observable behavior or text consistent with the bias.
3. State what process evidence is missing.
4. Offer at least one competing explanation.
5. Use the lowest warranted confidence.
6. Give a test or debiasing intervention.

Heuristics are not intrinsically irrational. A shortcut can be adaptive when information and time are costly or when it fits the environment. A single statement normally cannot establish a psychological process.

Keep phenomenon classes distinct:

- Cognitive bias: systematic judgment tendency.
- Heuristic: shortcut that may or may not produce bias.
- Memory effect: pattern in encoding, storage, or retrieval.
- Social effect: behavior changes under social conditions.
- Response or measurement bias: data-collection distortion.
- Clinical or psychobiological effect: not a reasoning diagnosis.
- Rhetorical tactic: potentially deliberate presentation choice.

## Bias inference levels

| Level | Label | Required evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | Not inferable | The statement alone supplies no process evidence. |
| 1 | Bias-consistent cue | A pattern is compatible with the bias, but ignorance, rhetoric, limited data, or a rational alternative remains plausible. This is the normal ceiling for text-only analysis. |
| 2 | Process-supported | Search history, changing standards, matched comparisons, or pre/post judgments reveal the predicted asymmetry. |
| 3 | Behaviorally demonstrated | Repeated or controlled observations satisfy a credible operational definition. |

Say "the evidence selection is consistent with confirmation bias" rather than "the speaker is confirmation-biased" at level 1.

## The 24 Your Bias Is entries

These tests cover all entries on [Your Bias Is](https://yourbias.is/) while correcting common popular overreach.

| Entry and class | Operational evidence | Guard and debiasing test |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fundamental attribution error — social judgment [YBI] | Trait explanations for others exceed those for matched self-behavior after situational constraints are held constant. | A trait may truly explain behavior. Swap actor and circumstances; list situational and dispositional evidence. |
| Placebo effect — psychobiological effect [YBI] | Outcomes differ between ritual/expectation conditions and suitable blinded controls. | Not text-detectable and not merely "imagining improvement." Use controlled comparison and distinguish subjective symptoms from disease mechanisms. |
| Reactance — motivational effect [YBI] | Preference or behavior shifts because freedom is perceived as restricted, independent of the proposal's merits. | Opposition may be rational because the restriction is coercive or harmful. Remove controlling language and reassess substance. |
| Optimism bias — forecasting [YBI] | Repeated forecasts are positively miscalibrated relative to base rates or outcomes. | Hope, ambition, or a risky choice is insufficient. Use reference-class forecasts and failure ranges. |
| Groupthink — group process [YBI] | Evidence of unanimity pressure, self-censorship, insulation, suppressed dissent, or failure to examine alternatives. | Consensus can result from shared evidence. Collect independent judgments before discussion and appoint a genuine challenger. |
| Belief bias — reasoning [YBI] | Judgments of logical validity change when an argument's conclusion becomes believable while form stays constant. | Believing a conclusion is not itself bias. Evaluate form before premise truth. |
| Availability heuristic — retrieval shortcut [YBI] | Ease of recall changes frequency or probability judgment beyond actual data. | Recent examples may be genuinely diagnostic. Compare with base rates and systematic records. |
| Spotlight effect — social metacognition [YBI] | Predicted observer attention exceeds observers' actual notice or recall. | Some conduct is highly visible. Ask what a neutral observer later remembers. |
| Pessimism bias — forecasting [YBI] | Repeated forecasts are negatively miscalibrated against defensible reference classes. | Prudence under asymmetric downside can be rational. Separate probability from risk tolerance. |
| Negativity bias — attention/evaluation [YBI] | Matched negative information receives more weight or persistence than positive information. | Negative evidence may carry greater diagnostic or survival relevance. Equalize relevance, not emotional valence. |
| Self-serving bias — attribution [YBI] | Own success is attributed internally and failure externally more than matched judgments of others. | Circumstances sometimes really differ. Predefine responsibility criteria and swap identities. |
| Curse of knowledge — communication [YBI] | A knowledgeable person overpredicts novice understanding after controlling what the novice was told. | An audience may possess shared expertise. Test with a real novice and elicit a paraphrase. |
| Just-world hypothesis — moral/social [YBI] | Deservedness or victim blame persists after conduct and causal responsibility are controlled. | Justice sometimes links choices and outcomes. Separate causation, culpability, and bad luck. |
| Declinism — trend judgment [YBI] | Deterioration claims conflict with appropriate longitudinal measures and rely on selective or rosy memory. | Some domains genuinely decline. Define metric, baseline, population, and time series. |
| Backfire effect — belief updating [YBI] | Belief in a false claim becomes stronger after correction than before or relative to control. | Persistence is not backfire, and generalized backfire is not robust. Measure belief before and after with comparison. |
| Confirmation bias — evidence handling [YBI] | Search, interpretation, standards, or recall systematically favor an existing belief. | Supporting evidence may simply be stronger. Precommit inclusion and apply identical tests to contrary evidence. |
| Anchoring — estimation [YBI] | Estimates shift with an initial value that should be irrelevant compared with another anchor or none. | Initial values can convey information. Generate an independent estimate before seeing the anchor. |
| Barnum effect — personal interpretation [YBI] | Generic feedback is judged uniquely accurate or personal, ideally when identical profiles are presented as individualized. | Some broad descriptions are genuinely informative. Compare applicability to randomly selected others. |
| Dunning–Kruger effect — calibration pattern [YBI] | Objective domain performance and self-assessment show low performers relatively overestimating and/or high performers underestimating. | Do not translate into "stupid people are confident." Account for regression, scale use, domain knowledge, and better-than-average effects. |
| Sunk-cost effect — decision [YBI] | Choice changes when irrecoverable past cost is varied while future consequences remain constant. | Past investment can inform future benefits or switching costs. Ask what to do if inheriting the situation today. |
| Framing effect — presentation [YBI] | Extensionally equivalent descriptions change judgment or preference. | Frames can reveal genuinely different goals or uncertainty. Rewrite in gains, losses, frequencies, and neutral terms. |
| In-group bias — social judgment [YBI] | Group labels change judgment when conduct and evidence are held constant. | Shared values or trust history can be relevant. Anonymize or swap labels. |
| Halo effect — cross-trait judgment [YBI] | An irrelevant favorable trait changes judgments on independent dimensions. | Traits can correlate. Blind irrelevant information and score dimensions separately. |
| Bystander effect — social behavior [YBI] | Intervention probability or speed changes with perceived presence of other helpers. | Not text-detectable; safety, competence, and role assignment matter. Assign a specific person and action in emergencies. |

## Evidence acquisition and attention

| Bias or effect | Bias-consistent pattern | Test or control |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Myside bias | Arguments and evidence are evaluated more favorably when supporting one's own side. | Blind the conclusion or reverse ideological labels. |
| Selective exposure | Sources are chosen mainly to avoid contrary information. | Define a balanced search protocol and include strongest opposition. |
| Positive-test strategy | Questions seek cases where the hypothesis is true rather than discriminating alternatives. | Ask which observation differs most across hypotheses. It is not irrational when positive tests are highly diagnostic. |
| Congruence bias | One hypothesis is tested without testing alternatives. | List at least two rivals and predictions unique to each. |
| Information avoidance / ostrich effect | Useful threatening information is delayed or ignored. | Precommit review dates and automatic reporting. |
| Attentional bias | Certain emotionally or motivationally salient stimuli capture disproportionate attention. | Compare matched stimuli and task relevance. |
| Salience bias | Conspicuous features dominate judgment despite weak relevance. | Score less visible variables before viewing salient presentation. |
| Mere exposure effect | Familiarity increases liking without added substantive value. | Compare blinded unfamiliar and familiar versions. |
| Illusory truth effect | Repetition increases perceived truth. | Track source and verify claim once; do not count repetitions as independent evidence. |
| Observer-expectancy effect | Researcher or evaluator expectations influence observation or interaction. | Blind, standardize, and independently code. |
| Experimenter demand | Participants alter behavior based on inferred study purpose. | Mask hypothesis and use indirect measures. |
| Automation bias | Automated output is accepted despite contrary evidence. | Require independent checks for high-impact cases and display uncertainty. |
| Authority bias | Deference exceeds what expertise, scope, or evidence warrants. | Separate expert testimony from command; compare qualified independent experts. |
| Focusing illusion | Attention to one aspect exaggerates its importance to overall welfare or outcome. | Enumerate the full value hierarchy and time allocation. |
| Identifiable-victim effect | One named case drives more response than equivalent or larger statistical harm. | Present both individual story and population totals. |
| Scope neglect | Response changes too little as the number or magnitude affected changes. | Use explicit quantities and marginal values. |
| Normalcy bias | Familiar normal conditions are projected despite credible evidence of disruption. | Use trigger thresholds and scenario drills. |

## Belief interpretation and updating

| Bias or effect | Bias-consistent pattern | Test or control |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Belief perseverance | Belief persists after its original evidential basis is discredited. | Rebuild the case from remaining evidence only. |
| Biased assimilation | Same mixed evidence is interpreted as supporting prior views. | Blind source and score methodology before results. |
| Disconfirmation bias | Contrary evidence receives more scrutiny than supporting evidence. | Apply one written quality rubric symmetrically. |
| Motivated reasoning | Desired conclusions alter search, standards, or interpretation. | State desired outcome, then use adversarial review and preregistered criteria. |
| Attitude polarization | Exposure to mixed evidence increases separation between prior groups. | Measure prior/posterior beliefs and force cross-side evidence ratings. |
| Continued-influence effect | Corrected misinformation continues to shape inference. | Supply a coherent replacement explanation and repeat correction in context. |
| Semmelweis reflex | New evidence is rejected reflexively because it conflicts with established norms. | Separate novelty from method and replicate. |
| Conservatism in updating | Beliefs adjust too little to diagnostic new evidence. | Calculate or explicitly estimate likelihood impact. |
| Overreaction | Beliefs adjust too much to noisy or recent evidence. | Use base rates, sample size, and shrinkage. |
| Anchoring and insufficient adjustment | Revision remains too close to an initial estimate. | Make outside-view estimate before reviewing prior number. |
| Affect heuristic | General positive or negative feeling substitutes for separate benefit and risk estimates. | Rate benefits and risks independently before integrating. |
| Horns effect | One negative trait depresses unrelated judgments. | Blind the trait and score dimensions independently. |
| Illusory correlation | A relationship is perceived because paired events are distinctive or expected. | Build a complete contingency table. |
| Naive realism | One's own perception is treated as direct reality while disagreement is blamed on bias or ignorance. | Reconstruct what evidence and perspective each party has. |
| Hostile-media effect | Balanced coverage is perceived as hostile by opposing partisans. | Blind source and use explicit content coding. |
| Reactive devaluation | A proposal is valued less because an opponent offered it. | Evaluate proposal without sponsor identity, then reveal source. |
| Moral credentialing | Prior good conduct is used to license later lower standards. | Judge each act by the same current principle. |
| Licensing effect | Prior progress or virtue increases indulgence inconsistent with the goal. | Track total trajectory, not earned permission. |
| Cognitive dissonance reduction | Beliefs shift to reduce conflict after an irrevocable choice or behavior. | Record beliefs before commitment and consider reversal counterfactually. |
| Wishful thinking | Desired outcome is assigned higher probability without evidence. | Separate utility from probability and use base rates. |
| Outcome-desirability bias | Evidence standards vary with how attractive the conclusion is. | Precommit threshold before learning direction. |

## Probability and uncertainty

| Bias or effect | Bias-consistent pattern | Test or control |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Base-rate neglect | Case detail overwhelms background frequency. | State prior frequency before individuating evidence. |
| Representativeness heuristic | Similarity to a prototype substitutes for probability. | Check prevalence, sample process, and predictive validity. |
| Conjunction error | A detailed A-and-B story feels more likely than A. | Compare nested probabilities explicitly. |
| Denominator neglect / ratio bias | Larger numerator feels more likely despite smaller proportion. | Convert to common denominators or percentages. |
| Sample-size neglect | Extreme results in small samples are treated as equally stable. | Report uncertainty by n. |
| Law of small numbers | Small samples are expected to match population distribution. | Simulate or calculate sampling variation. |
| Clustering illusion | Random streaks or clusters are overinterpreted. | Compare with null distribution and account for search. |
| Gambler's fallacy | Opposite result is judged due in an independent process. | Verify independence and compute conditional probability. |
| Hot-hand belief or hot-hand neglect | Serial dependence is asserted or dismissed without correct analysis. | Test actual conditional performance while correcting selection bias. |
| Regression neglect | Extreme cases are expected to remain extreme or change is credited causally. | Use repeated baseline and comparison. |
| Probability neglect | Magnitude of probability receives too little weight relative to emotion. | Display absolute frequencies and expected consequences. |
| Zero-risk bias | Eliminating a small risk is preferred over a larger total risk reduction. | Compare absolute expected harm and cost. |
| Certainty effect | Certain outcomes receive disproportionate weight relative to near-certain ones. | Compare expected values and utility under equivalent frames. |
| Pseudocertainty effect | A conditional outcome is perceived as certain because earlier uncertainty is hidden. | Show the full decision tree. |
| Ambiguity effect | Options with unknown probabilities are avoided beyond warranted uncertainty cost. | Bound probabilities and value information. |
| Neglect of prior probability | Posterior judgment ignores plausible starting odds. | Use explicit Bayesian or frequency reasoning. |
| Insensitivity to predictability | Confident forecasts are made from descriptions with weak predictive power. | Demand validation against outcomes. |
| Overprecision | Confidence intervals are too narrow. | Require ranges and score calibration. |
| Overestimation | Ability, probability of success, or performance is overpredicted. | Compare forecast with base rate and historical outcomes. |
| Overplacement | Rank relative to others is overpredicted. | Use objective peer distribution. |
| Hard-easy effect | Confidence is too high on hard tasks and too low on easy tasks. | Plot confidence against accuracy by difficulty. |
| Illusion of control | Personal influence over random or uncontrollable outcomes is exaggerated. | Identify actual causal lever and compare randomized conditions. |
| Illusion of validity | Coherent input generates confidence beyond predictive accuracy. | Track out-of-sample calibration. |
| Survivorship judgment | Probability is estimated only from visible successes. | Reconstruct failures and initial cohort. |

## Choice, value, and time

| Bias or effect | Bias-consistent pattern | Test or control |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Loss aversion | Equivalent losses receive more weight than gains beyond the person's stable value context. | Frame both ways and examine reference point; effect is context-sensitive. |
| Endowment effect | Ownership raises valuation without changing use value or alternatives. | Compare willingness to pay and accept while controlling transaction costs and attachment. |
| Status-quo bias | Existing option is favored beyond switching costs and uncertainty. | Treat each option as if choosing fresh today. |
| Default effect | Preselected option changes choice absent substantive information. | Present active choice and disclose default rationale. |
| Sunk-cost escalation | Irrecoverable investment increases continued commitment. | Ignore past cost except where it predicts future consequences. |
| Escalation of commitment | Negative feedback leads to greater investment to justify prior choice or identity. | Use independent review and preset exit criteria. |
| Present bias | Immediate outcomes receive dynamically excessive weight over future outcomes. | Use precommitment and compare choices from a common future date. |
| Hyperbolic discounting | Discount rate changes as outcomes approach, producing preference reversals. | Commit while both outcomes remain future. |
| Projection bias | Current tastes or state are projected into future preferences. | Use observations across states and cooling-off periods. |
| Empathy gap | Judgment made in one visceral state underestimates behavior in another. | Plan while calm for hunger, anger, fear, pain, or arousal. |
| Planning fallacy | Own project time or cost is underestimated despite comparable history. | Use reference-class data and decompose unknowns. |
| Impact bias | Intensity or duration of future emotion is overestimated. | Compare with adaptation data and past events. |
| Focalism | One future change is imagined while other life events and adaptations are omitted. | Simulate the full future day or week. |
| Omission bias | Harm from inaction is weighted less than equivalent harm from action. | Compare outcomes and causal responsibility symmetrically. |
| Action bias | Action is preferred merely to avoid appearing passive. | Include no-action as a deliberate option with triggers. |
| Ambiguity aversion | Known-risk option is preferred even when uncertain option may be better. | Value information and bound worst/best cases. |
| Regret aversion | Avoiding anticipated regret dominates objective value. | Separate decision quality from outcome luck. |
| Decoy effect | Inferior third option shifts preference between two main options. | Compare each option to fixed goals without the decoy. |
| Compromise effect | Middle option is favored because it is intermediate. | Score absolute fit to needs. |
| Attraction/asymmetric-dominance effect | A dominated option increases choice of its dominator. | Remove dominated options and reassess. |
| Mental accounting | Fungible resources are treated differently by arbitrary account labels. | Evaluate total portfolio and real constraints. |
| Disposition effect | Winners are sold too early and losers held too long relative to prospects. | Ignore purchase price and reassess future expected value and taxes. |
| Choice-supportive bias | Chosen option's virtues and rejected option's defects are exaggerated after choice. | Preserve pre-choice notes and conduct postmortem. |
| IKEA / effort-valuation effect | Self-created items are valued more because of effort. | Blind creator and compare market alternatives. |
| Less-is-better effect | A smaller set with high average quality is valued above a larger set containing it when evaluated separately. | Evaluate jointly on total value and goals. |
| Peak-end rule | Memory of an experience is dominated by peak and ending rather than full duration. | Record moment-by-moment and total outcomes. |
| Duration neglect | Experience length receives insufficient weight. | Include aggregate duration and intensity. |
| Choice overload | More options are assumed to impair choice. | Verify in this context; effects depend on complexity, preferences, and support. |
| Scarcity heuristic | Scarcity is treated as evidence of value. | Identify actual utility and reason for scarcity. |
| Effort justification | Greater effort increases valuation independent of result. | Compare outcome quality while hiding effort. |

## Self and social judgment

| Bias or effect | Bias-consistent pattern | Test or control |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Actor-observer asymmetry | Own behavior receives situational explanations while others receive dispositional ones. | Swap roles; note that broad claims about this effect are empirically mixed. |
| Ultimate attribution error | Out-group negative acts are attributed to character and positive acts to circumstance, reversed for in-group. | Swap group labels and code explanations. |
| Better-than-average effect | Most individuals place themselves above median on traits where that is impossible. | Use objective distribution and defined comparison group. |
| Illusory superiority | Ability or virtue is overranked without performance evidence. | Use blind external measures. |
| Worse-than-average effect | Self is underrated on difficult or rare skills relative to peers. | Compare actual distribution. |
| Bias blind spot | Others' bias is recognized more readily than one's own. | Require the same process audit for self and opponent. |
| Introspection illusion | Access to one's reasons is treated as proof that unconscious or situational influences are absent. | Compare behavior under manipulated conditions. |
| False-consensus effect | One's beliefs or behavior are assumed more common than data show. | Use representative surveys and clear reference group. |
| False-uniqueness effect | Desirable traits or behavior are assumed rarer among others than they are. | Measure actual prevalence. |
| In-group favoritism | Equivalent in-group members receive more resources or benefit of doubt. | Anonymize membership. |
| Out-group homogeneity | Out-group members are perceived as more alike than in-group members. | Sample individual variation and remove labels. |
| Group polarization | Group discussion shifts members toward a more extreme version of their initial tendency. | Collect independent pre/post judgments and expose contrary arguments. |
| Conformity effect | Judgment changes toward group response without new evidence. | Elicit private independent answers first. |
| Bandwagon effect | Adoption increases because others adopted, beyond informational or coordination value. | Hide popularity and evaluate merits. |
| Pluralistic ignorance | Individuals privately reject a norm but assume others accept it and comply publicly. | Use anonymous preference collection. |
| Diffusion of responsibility | Personal action decreases as perceived number of responsible others grows. | Assign a named owner and deadline. |
| Bystander effect | Help changes with other observers' presence. | Direct a specific person to act. |
| Social loafing | Individual effort declines when contribution is less identifiable in a group. | Define ownership and measure contributions. |
| Authority bias | Authority status outweighs relevant evidence. | Require rationale and dissent channel. |
| System-justification tendency | Existing arrangements are defended as natural or deserved beyond evidence. | Imagine the same system newly proposed or roles reversed. |
| Just-world belief | Outcome is treated as evidence of desert. | Separate luck, causation, choice, and justice. |
| Stereotyping | Group generalization substitutes for individual evidence. | Use relevant base rates cautiously and update with individual data. |
| Beauty premium / attractiveness halo | Appearance changes unrelated competence or character judgments. | Blind visual identity where irrelevant. |
| Identifiable-victim effect | Named individuals receive disproportionate weight. | Pair narrative with aggregate comparison. |
| Social-desirability bias | Reported attitudes or conduct shift toward perceived approval. | Use anonymity, indirect questions, and behavioral measures. |
| Courtesy bias | Respondent suppresses criticism to avoid offense. | Use private channels and neutral wording. |
| Acquiescence bias | Respondent tends to agree regardless of item content. | Balance reverse-worded items carefully. |
| Demand characteristics | Behavior reflects guessed evaluator expectations. | Mask purpose and separate researcher. |
| Third-person effect | Others are assumed more influenced by media than oneself. | Apply symmetric evidence and experimental data. |
| Spotlight effect | Others' attention to self is overestimated. | Measure actual recall. |
| Illusion of transparency | Internal thoughts or emotions are assumed more visible than they are. | Ask observers what they inferred. |
| Curse of knowledge | One's knowledge is projected onto others. | Test novice comprehension. |

## Memory and hindsight

| Bias or effect | Bias-consistent pattern | Test or control |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hindsight bias | After learning an outcome, prior predictability or one's earlier forecast is inflated. | Preserve timestamped predictions and reasons. |
| Outcome bias | Decision quality is judged by result while ignoring information and probabilities available at choice time. | Reconstruct ex ante context. |
| Rosy retrospection | Past experiences are remembered more positively than contemporaneous reports. | Compare diaries with later recall. |
| Consistency bias | Past attitudes are remembered as more like current attitudes. | Use prior records. |

| Illusory truth | Familiar repeated statements feel truer. | Verify source rather than familiarity. |
| Primacy effect | Early items receive greater recall or influence. | Counterbalance order. |

| Availability in memory | Easily recalled events distort frequency judgment. | Use systematic counts. |

| Telescoping | Dates are remembered as more recent or distant than they were. | Check timestamped records. |
| Fading-affect pattern | Negative emotional intensity fades differently from positive. | Separate remembered fact from present affect. |

## Metacognition and problem solving

| Bias or effect | Bias-consistent pattern | Test or control |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Overconfidence | Confidence exceeds accuracy across repeated judgments. | Use proper scoring and calibration curves. |
| Dunning–Kruger pattern | Self-assessment varies systematically with performance. | Measure domain skill and avoid personality conclusions. |
| Illusion of explanatory depth | Understanding feels deep until a mechanism must be explained step by step. | Ask for a causal diagram or operational explanation before and after rating. |
| Processing-fluency effect | Easy-to-read or familiar claims receive greater truth or quality ratings. | Standardize format and verify substance. |
| Cognitive ease | Low effort is treated as evidence of correctness. | Introduce independent checks; difficulty alone also is not evidence. |
| Einstellung effect | A familiar solution blocks a simpler or better method. | Generate alternatives before applying the standard tool. |
| Functional fixedness | An object's conventional use limits recognition of other uses. | Describe components and properties without names. |
| Law of the instrument | A familiar tool or theory is applied to every problem. | Start with problem requirements and compare methods. |
| Automation complacency | Monitoring declines because automation is expected to work. | Design active cross-checks and failure drills. |
| Automation surprise | Operator's mental model diverges from automated state. | Expose modes, transitions, and intent. |
| Restraint bias | Ability to resist temptation or pressure is overestimated. | Reduce exposure and precommit rather than relying on willpower. |
| Planning fallacy | Own schedule ignores comparable project outcomes. | Use outside-view distribution. |
| Not-invented-here bias | External ideas are undervalued because of origin. | Blind provenance and compare performance. |
| Pro-innovation bias | Novelty's benefits are overstated and limitations underweighted. | Compare with baseline, adoption costs, and failure cases. |
| Status-quo bias | Existing method is favored without full comparison. | Rechoose from scratch. |
| Complexity bias | Complex explanations or solutions are preferred because they appear sophisticated. | Choose the simplest model that explains and predicts evidence, not simplistic slogans. |
| Simplicity bias | A simpler story is preferred despite missing facts or poorer predictive fit. | Compare explanatory adequacy, not elegance alone. |
| Narrative bias | Coherent story receives more confidence than fragmented but stronger evidence. | Score evidence before constructing narrative. |
| Intentionality bias | Events are overattributed to deliberate agents. | Compare accident, incentives, systems, and emergent alternatives. |
| Anthropomorphism | Nonhuman or collective systems are assigned human intentions. | Reduce to actual mechanisms and agents. |

## Empirical-status cautions

Do not treat a popular label as a universal law.

- Backfire effect: persistence after correction is common, but increased belief caused by correction is difficult to elicit and not a general response.
- Dunning–Kruger: retain the domain-specific calibration meaning; regression, scale boundaries, and better-than-average effects can contribute.
- Actor-observer asymmetry: broad universal formulations have mixed meta-analytic support.
- Hot hand: do not assume independence; corrected analyses can find real serial dependence in some skilled performance.
- Loss aversion and endowment: effects vary by goods, reference points, experience, and design.
- Choice overload: not an automatic consequence of more options.
- Groupthink: a historical case label is not a diagnosis without group-process evidence.
- Bystander and social effects: context, danger, role clarity, competence, and communication alter results.
- Social and behavioral priming: demand current, replicated evidence for the precise effect.
- Ego depletion and broad decision-fatigue claims: do not include as established without current domain-specific evidence.
- Implicit-association measures: do not infer an individual's stable prejudice or conduct from one score.
- Heuristics: consider ecological rationality, information cost, and goals before calling the shortcut irrational.

When a bias claim is consequential, consult current review or meta-analytic research rather than relying only on this catalog.

## Debiasing methods

Choose a control matched to the mechanism:

### Evidence and belief

- Define falsifiers before searching.
- Consider the opposite and generate rival hypotheses.
- Use the same evidence rubric for favored and opposed claims.
- Blind ideological, group, or author labels.
- Separate discovery data from confirmation data.
- Preserve a source trail and count independent origins.

### Probability and forecasting

- Use relevant base rates and reference classes.
- Express probabilities as natural frequencies.
- Record forecasts with dates, confidence, and resolution criteria.
- Score calibration and update from outcomes.
- Use ranges, sensitivity analysis, and failure thresholds.

### Decisions

- Define objectives and criteria before seeing options.
- Ignore irrecoverable cost except as evidence of future consequences.
- Reframe gains and losses symmetrically.
- Use a cooling-off period when affect is high.
- Precommit stop rules and independent review.
- Compare the decision as if inherited today.

### Groups

- Collect independent estimates before discussion.
- Make dissent safe and specific.
- Assign a devil's advocate who genuinely develops the countercase.
- Rotate speaking order and prevent authority from speaking first.
- Record rejected alternatives and why.
- Assign a named owner in emergencies.

### Self-audit

- Run the same critique after reversing parties or conclusions.
- Ask what evidence would change the belief.
- Separate probability from desirability.
- Keep a decision journal.
- Seek the strongest competent critic, not the easiest opponent.
- Inspect emotional appraisals without treating emotion as proof.

Debiasing can itself create overcorrection. The goal is proportionate judgment, not reflexively choosing the opposite.

## Reporting

For each material finding:

- Bias or effect.
- Phenomenon class.
- Normative benchmark.
- Target: source process, speaker, audience, analyst, or user.
- Inference level 0–3.
- Observable indicator.
- Missing process evidence.
- Competing explanations.
- Empirical-status caveat.
- Debiasing test.
- Material impact if present.

Always include "not a diagnosis" for text-only findings.
