## Derivation and standard

Ethics asks why values are needed and which values a human being should choose. The Objectivist line of analysis begins with living organisms:

1. Life is a self-sustaining process that faces an alternative: continuation or death.
2. A living organism must act to gain and keep conditions its life requires.
3. A human being does not possess an automatic code sufficient for long-range survival and flourishing.
4. Human life therefore requires conceptual knowledge, chosen values, and principles.
5. The objective standard is the factual requirements of a rational human life across a lifespan.
6. The individual's own life and happiness are the moral purpose; values and virtues identify how to achieve them.

Do not present this as a verbal deduction from an axiom. Each bridge depends on observation of organisms, human nature, volition, social existence, and long-range causation.

"Life" here is neither bare momentary survival nor maximum longevity at any price. It means the integrated life proper to a rational being: functioning by reason, pursuing values, exercising agency, sustaining character and relationships, and achieving noncontradictory happiness over time.

## Rational egoism

Rational self-interest means acting for one's objective, long-range flourishing by principles grounded in human nature and facts. It excludes:

- Doing whatever one feels.
- Grabbing short-term gain while destroying larger values or character.
- Exploiting, deceiving, coercing, or sacrificing others.
- Material accumulation without a rational hierarchy of values.
- Treating other people as irrelevant.
- Treating every refusal to self-sacrifice as wise.

Ask:

- Whose life is the beneficiary and over what time horizon?
- Are the proposed values mutually compatible?
- What knowledge, character, relationships, health, productive capacity, and future options are affected?
- Does the action depend on another person's victimization or unearned sacrifice?
- Would the principle remain life-serving if practiced consistently in relevantly similar cases?

## Values and virtues

### Cardinal values

- Reason: the means of knowledge and fundamental guide to action.
- Purpose: the productive direction that organizes effort and a value hierarchy.
- Self-esteem: earned confidence in one's efficacy and worthiness to live and be happy.

### Rationality

Accept responsibility for focusing, identifying facts, integrating knowledge, and acting on reality rather than evasion. Rationality is the primary virtue on which the others depend.

Audit:

- What facts does the actor need to know?
- Has effort been proportioned to stakes?
- Is a contradiction being evaded?
- Is authority, faith, or emotion replacing judgment?

### Independence

Accept primary responsibility for one's judgments and livelihood. Learning from experts, collaborating, receiving help, and dividing cognitive labor can be independent when one judges whom and how to rely on.

Do not confuse independence with isolation, reflexive contrarianism, or refusing earned assistance.

### Integrity

Act consistently with rational convictions and principles. Integrity does not sanctify a false principle; update convictions when evidence defeats them.

### Honesty

Refuse to fake reality to oneself or others. The central harm is dependence on an unreal picture of facts. Context-sensitive privacy, confidentiality, silence, tactical concealment from an aggressor, and refusing an intrusive question are not automatically dishonest.

### Justice

Judge people by relevant evidence and grant what they deserve. Consider actions, character, knowledge, context, benefits, harms, and chosen responsibility. Justice rejects both unearned condemnation and unearned praise.

### Productiveness

Create the material and spiritual values human life requires by shaping nature to a rational purpose. Productiveness is broader than paid employment and is not measured solely by income or social status.

### Pride

Practice moral ambitiousness: shape one's character, correct defects, pursue excellence, and earn self-esteem. Pride is not boasting, narcissism, prestige seeking, or pretending to virtues not earned.

## Other people, trade, and benevolence

### Trader principle

Deal with others by voluntary exchange of value for value, material or spiritual. The exchanged values need not be identical or monetary. Friendship, love, mentoring, generosity, and cooperation can be profoundly selfish when they reflect genuine values rather than duty to self-erasure.

### No inherent conflicts among rational interests

Objectivism rejects the view that rational people normally prosper by preying on one another. Apparent conflicts require analysis:

- Are both goals objectively rational?
- Is there a scarce indivisible value that only one can obtain?
- Is force, fraud, breach, or unearned demand present?
- Does losing a competition equal being sacrificed?
- Is compromise available without surrendering principle?

"No conflict" does not mean every person gets every desired concrete. It means another person's rational flourishing is not inherently a threat or resource to be consumed.

### Benevolence and generosity

Helping another can express the helper's values, affection, solidarity, emergency concern, or investment in a good world. The ethical question is not whether another benefits, but whether the action rationally serves the giver's hierarchy without treating self-sacrifice as a duty.

### Love and friendship

Personal relationships are selfish values when one loves actual virtues, character, shared values, and the person's irreplaceable significance. Avoid both duty-bound attachment and instrumental exploitation.

## Emergencies and context

An emergency is an abnormal, temporary condition in which human life is threatened and normal value production or trade is disrupted. Emergency ethics should not be generalized into a standing social code.

Audit:

- Is the condition genuinely exceptional, urgent, and temporary?
- What values are at risk and what action is feasible?
- Did the agent create the emergency?
- Is aid minor relative to the value protected or a destruction of the agent's own life?
- Are normal principles suspended, narrowed, or simply applied under unusual facts?

Do not call every hardship an emergency. Do not infer that because aid is rational in an emergency, permanent sacrificial obligation follows.

## Moral judgment

Separate:

- Factual error.
- Epistemic negligence.
- Honest uncertainty.
- Vice or evasion.
- Harmful action.
- Rights violation.
- Legal liability.

Moral judgment requires evidence of the act, the choices available, knowledge reasonably accessible, time and stakes, pattern, consequences, and the actor's response to correction. Avoid omniscient retrospective standards.

Moral evaluation can be decisive without declaring a person wholly good or evil. Judge the scope and centrality of the evidence.

## Ethics audit

For a value claim or proposed action, answer:

1. What is the chosen value and who is meant to benefit?
2. What facts of human nature make it a value or disvalue?
3. What time horizon and wider value hierarchy apply?
4. What alternative actions exist?
5. Which virtues or vices does the action embody?
6. Does it require force, fraud, sacrifice, pretense, or contradiction?
7. What predictable direct and indirect consequences follow?
8. Is the judgment about prudence, morality, character, or politics?
9. What context could reverse or qualify the evaluation?
10. What principle can be stated without dropping that context?

Use this derivation:

facts about entity and circumstances
→ relationship to rational human life
→ value or disvalue
→ virtue or action principle
→ contextual evaluation

## Frequent errors

- Whim worship: Calling any desire selfish and therefore good.
- Short-range collapse: Ignoring future character, trust, health, knowledge, or options.
- Predatory egoism: Treating another person's loss or coercion as the essence of gain.
- Materialism: Reducing flourishing to money, goods, or rank.
- Duty inversion: Assuming help is altruistic and therefore irrational regardless of the helper's values.
- Sacrifice equivocation: Calling every cost, tradeoff, effort, or foregone alternative a sacrifice.
- Virtue intrinsicism: Applying honesty, integrity, or independence as contextless commandments.
- Outcome-only judgment: Ignoring method, evidence, choice, and foreseeable risk.
- Intention-only judgment: Ignoring actual means and consequences.
- Moralism: Inferring vice from one disagreement or mistake.
- Politics leakage: Treating immoral conduct as automatically subject to legal prohibition.
- Collective beneficiary: Speaking of "society's good" without reducing it to the lives and rights of individuals.
