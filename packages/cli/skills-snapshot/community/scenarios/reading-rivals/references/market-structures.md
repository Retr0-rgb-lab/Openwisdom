## Price prisoner's dilemma

```
                    rival hold    rival cut
        us hold      100, 100      55, 130
        us cut       130,  55      70,  70
```

Cutting is dominant for both. Both cut. Both end at 70, worse than the 100 they would
each have had by holding. Nobody made a mistake at any step.

**Recognising it:** cutting beats holding for you regardless of what they do, and the
same is true for them, and the both-cut cell is worse for both than the both-hold cell.

**What does not work:** signalling restraint through blog posts, waiting them out,
matching quickly to "show strength". All of these play the game and arrive at the same
cell.

**What works:** change the cells. Switching costs and annual contracts raise the payoff
of holding by making a cut less effective at moving customers. Segmenting so you are not
competing on the same axis removes the dilemma. Public, committed pricing removes your
own ability to cut, which can make holding credible for both sides.

**The commercial fact behind it:** a price cut is easy to match and hard to reverse,
which is precisely the structure that produces this table. Almost every price cut in a
two-player market ends here.

## Feature race

Both sides gain by shipping the feature the other has. Neither gains by shipping first,
because the other matches within a quarter, and both end up carrying more surface area
at the same relative position.

**Recognising it:** your roadmap is mostly parity items, and each launch is followed by
their matching launch and then the reverse.

**Why it persists:** each individual match is locally rational. The cumulative maintenance
cost is not visible in any single decision, which is exactly the structure the
**learning-from-outcomes** skill's credit assignment is for.

**What works:** stop matching the ones that do not convert. Measure whether the parity
feature actually changed close rates before building the next one. In a race, refusing to
run is only viable if you can point at evidence that the feature does not matter, and
that evidence has to be collected deliberately.

## Standards or platform coordination

Both gain by picking the same standard, format or integration, and either choice beats
splitting. Two equilibria, both good, and the difficulty is coordinating on one.

**Recognising it:** the payoff table's diagonal is high and the off-diagonal is low, for
both players.

**What works:** move first and publicly. In a coordination game, a visible commitment is
an advantage rather than a vulnerability, because it lets the other side coordinate onto
you. This is the one structure where announcing your plan is strictly good.

## Land grab

The market has a fixed number of positions - integration slots, marketplace listings,
category mindshare - and taking one denies it to the other side. Genuinely close to
zero-sum, unlike most software competition.

**Recognising it:** the resource is countable and finite, and there is a deadline.

**What works:** speed, and accepting worse unit economics during the grab, because the
position is worth more than the margin on the transaction that captures it. This is the
one situation where growth-at-any-cost is the correct strategy rather than a funding
artefact.

**The failure mode:** believing you are in a land grab when you are not. Most software
markets are not fixed-size, and treating a growing market as a land grab destroys margin
for a position that was never scarce.

## Asymmetric attention

You model them carefully. They have not heard of you.

**Recognising it:** large size difference, no observable response to any of your moves.

**What works:** ignore them strategically and copy them tactically. Do not model a rival
who is not modelling you as a strategic actor - it overstates the threat and, worse, an
aggressive move aimed at them may be the thing that puts you on their radar and creates
the competitor you were worried about.

**The exception:** if your move would be visible to them and material to their business,
you have converted this into a real game. Check that before making it.

## Reading which one you are in

Three questions, in order:

1. **Is the pie fixed?** Yes points at land grab or zero-sum. No, which is the usual
   answer, rules out most aggressive framings immediately.
2. **Can they match your move, and how fast?** Fast and cheap points at prisoner's
   dilemma or feature race. Slow or expensive means you have a real position.
3. **Do you both win if you do the same thing?** Yes points at coordination, and
   coordination is the one shape where you should move first and loudly.

If the answers are "no, fast, no", you are in a prisoner's dilemma or a feature race, and
the productive work is changing the game rather than playing it better.
